# Fire Aboard Space Station Mir (Feb. 24, 1997)

On Feb. 24, 1997, the six crew members aboard the Russian space station Mir faced significant danger when a fire ignited in an oxygen generating system. The flame, lasting several minutes, cut off access to one of the two Soyuz escape vehicles and filled the station's modules with smoke. Through teamwork the crew extinguished the flames, with no damage to the station's structure. Life support cleared the toxic smoke over several hours, with no lasting harm to the crew. Lessons learned were passed on to the design and operations of the International Space Station.

## Crew and event
Mir normally housed three, but during crew handovers it held six. Aboard: Valeri G. Korzun, Aleksandr Y. Kaleri (Russia), NASA astronaut Jerry M. Linenger, and newly arrived Vasili V. Tsibliyev, Aleksandr I. Lazutkin (Roscosmos) and Reinhold Ewald (German space agency DLR). Shortly after dinner, as Lazutkin activated a solid-fuel oxygen generator in the Kvant-1 module, the canister ignited, shooting a three-foot flame with sparks and bits of molten metal across the module and generating dense smoke. The flame blocked the route to the Soyuz docked at the aft of Kvant-1. The crew donned oxygen masks; Korzun doused the flame with foam from a fire extinguisher while Linenger held him in place and others handed him two more extinguishers. The flame finally extinguished itself; some Kvant-1 panels were charred. It was the worst in-orbit fire incident in the history of spaceflight.

## Oxygen systems and cause
Normally two Elektron units generated oxygen by electrolysis of (mostly reclaimed) water. With larger crews, solid fuel oxygen generators (SFOG, also called Vika or TGK) supplemented them; they burn canisters of solid lithium perchlorate, each providing enough oxygen for one crew member for one day. SFOGs had been used without incident more than 2,500 times aboard Mir. After two years of study, investigators determined that hydrocarbons introduced into the canister increased the risk; the most likely culprit was latex working-glove material inadvertently introduced into the canister.

## Lessons learned
Russian officials changed emergency training for crews and how ground controllers support the onboard crew, and changed ground preparation of SFOG canisters. Today the ISS has dissimilar redundant oxygen generation: three improved Elektron units in the Russian segment and the Oxygen Generation System (electrolysis) in the U.S. segment; an SFOG system with additional safety measures is available in the Russian segment for larger crews or as backup.
