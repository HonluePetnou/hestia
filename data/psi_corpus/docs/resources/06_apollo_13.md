# Apollo 13

## Mission and crew
After Apollo 11 and 12, NASA set out to complete a third lunar landing with Apollo 13, targeting the Fra Mauro region. Crew: Commander James A. Lovell Jr., Lunar Module Pilot Fred W. Haise Jr., Command Module Pilot John L. "Jack" Swigert Jr. (who replaced Ken Mattingly after a German measles exposure; Mattingly lacked immunity). During launch, the center engine of the Saturn V S-II second stage shut down about two minutes early; the remaining four engines burned 34 seconds longer and the S-IVB third stage nine seconds longer to reach orbit. Pre-launch ground tests had indicated the possibility of a poorly insulated helium tank in the Lunar Module and the previously damaged oxygen tank number two.

## The accident
On the third day, shortly after a 49-minute TV broadcast, the crew heard a dull bang. One to two seconds later, warning lights indicated the loss of two of the three fuel cells, prompting Swigert's "Houston, we've had a problem." About 14 minutes later, Lovell saw the spacecraft venting oxygen into space. The explosion in oxygen tank number two also damaged tank number one, making the fuel cells unusable and threatening the loss of all electrical power and water. With only 15 minutes of power left in the Command Module (CM), the crew moved to "lifeboat" mode in the Lunar Module (LM) Aquarius, which had a full oxygen tank and descent engine. Flight Director Eugene Kranz set the new mission: abandon the landing, loop around the Moon, and get home as quickly as possible.

## Survival problems
- Carbon dioxide removal: the CM's square lithium hydroxide canisters were not compatible with the LM's round openings; Houston devised a method to make them work.
- Water: crew cut down to six ounces each per day (1/5 normal intake).
- Cold: with systems off, the spacecraft dropped to 38 °F and condensation formed on the walls.
- Re-powering the CM: Houston wrote in three days a procedure that normally takes three months. Thanks to the post-Apollo 1 redesign that added insulation to the wiring, the condensation did not cause arcing/short circuits.
- Navigation: a 35-second burn five hours after the explosion put the spacecraft back on a free-return trajectory; a five-minute burn followed after rounding the Moon. Splashdown in the Pacific on April 17.

## Cause (Apollo 13 Review Board)
In 1965 the CM was modified to raise the permissible voltage to the oxygen tank heaters from 28 to 65 volts DC, but the heaters' thermostatic switches were not modified to suit this change. During the final test on the launch pad, the heaters were left on for an extended period and reached about 1000 °F, degrading the Teflon insulation. The tank, damaged by eight hours of overheating, was left vulnerable the next time it was filled with oxygen. On June 15, 1970, Review Board Chair Edgar M. Cortright released the final report: the accident was a combination of mistakes and a deficient design (seven weeks of investigation, four panels).
