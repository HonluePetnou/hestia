# Apollo 1 (AS-204)

## Mission
The Apollo 1 mission was set to be the first crewed flight of the Apollo program and was scheduled to launch on Feb. 21, 1967. It would be a 14-day Earth orbital test flight of a Block I Apollo Command and Service Module (CSM). The primary objectives included testing the spacecraft's life support systems and the main Service Propulsion System (SPS) engine. Crew: Virgil "Gus" Grissom (Commander), Edward White (Senior Pilot), Roger Chaffee (Pilot). NASA announced the crew on March 21, 1966. Construction of Command Module 012 began in Downey, California, in August 1964 (North American Aviation). The spacecraft arrived at Kennedy on Aug. 26, 1966, completed altitude chamber tests in late December 1966, and was moved to Launch Pad 34 on Jan. 3, 1967.

## The accident
On Jan. 27, 1967, Grissom, White and Chaffee were conducting a key test on the launch pad, during which the spacecraft would switch to its own internal power. The astronauts wore spacesuits with the spacecraft's hatches closed and the cabin pressurized with 100% oxygen. During the test, a flash fire swept through the spacecraft so quickly there was no time to open the hatches. Within seconds, rising temperatures and pressure caused the Command Module pressure vessel to crack, releasing flames and smoke onto the launch pad's service structure. The astronauts attempted to open the inner hatch while the pad crew tried to open the two outer hatches. By the time the pad crew opened the hatches, the crew had already perished. U.S. Air Force pathologists concluded the astronauts died of asphyxia from carbon monoxide and other toxic gases resulting from the fire; their burns were likely survivable.

## Investigation
On Jan. 28, 1967, Deputy NASA Administrator Robert C. Seamans established the Apollo 204 Review Board, which established 21 panels. On April 9, 1967, the board released its 3,000-page report. Although they couldn't determine a specific source of ignition, investigators found evidence of arcing in several wires near the environment control system, the area with the most severe fire damage. Because of the pure oxygen atmosphere, the fire spread quickly, causing a rapid rise in temperature and pressure that led to the rupture of the Command Module pressure vessel. This rupture generated toxic chemicals that caused the deaths of the astronauts. The three-piece hatch took 90 seconds to open under ideal conditions and was impossible to open under increased pressure. Since the test took place with an unfueled rocket, it was considered non-hazardous and not reviewed by appropriate safety organizations; there was no defined process for rescuing or treating the crew. The board determined that both technical and management lapses led to the accident; both houses of Congress held hearings.

## Lessons learned / changes
- All subsequent Apollo crews flew aboard the Block II spacecraft (docking probe and transfer tunnel).
- The three-piece hatch was replaced with a unified hatch that could be opened from inside or outside within three seconds.
- NASA severely restricted and controlled the amount and location of combustible materials in the spacecraft, and made those changes in the Lunar Module as well.
- New spacesuits were designed using fire-resistant materials.
- Management and Safety Culture changes.
- (From the Apollo 13 page) a major redesign after Apollo 1 added increased insulation to better protect wiring, which later prevented arcing when Apollo 13's cold, condensation-covered Command Module was powered back up.
